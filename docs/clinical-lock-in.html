Primary clinical narrative

Clinical Lock-In

This case is no longer sensibly reduced to whether one man's foot pain was “Parkinsonian.” The real issue is whether an early neurological–foot boundary became embedded in care and was not adequately revised when later evidence repeatedly crossed it.

Read the expert-facing companion →

The problem is not that medicine tolerated uncertainty. The problem is that the founding synthesis was epistemically uncertain but operationally committed.

In July 2017 Neurology documented a strikingly abnormal gait, marked rigidity and bradykinesia, a lower-limb appearance “somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture – NOT FOOT DROP,” abnormal feet, and an “almost dystonically unusual” walk while suspecting autosomal-recessive Parkinsonism. PRKN/PARK2 reasoning later became sophisticated. Yet the painful feet were left compatible with “completely separate,” possible peripheral neuropathy and “Not clear,” then routed toward Podiatry without a clearly documented neurological question or return mechanism.

The claimant does not allege that Podiatry was inappropriate or that every foot lesion was neurological. The question is whether Neurology had adequately determined, or established a process capable of determining, what was structural, what was mechanically secondary, what was neurologically driven or modulated, and how those components interacted.

The Protest

The patient's account is that, immediately before discharge, he said in substance: “You have not dealt with my feet.” That remains a factual allegation requiring proof. If accepted, it becomes a decision-after-notice question: did reasonable neurological practice require the unresolved relationship to be reopened before closure?

The founding communication then matters because it presents the admission as unusually extensive — prolonged assessment, exploration of many questions, Complex Case Meeting, senior neurological input and a “firmer footing going forward.” If the Protest occurred, omission of that challenge could allow challenged incompleteness to travel downstream as apparently considered specialist uncertainty.

Treatment reassures the patient and challenges the formulation

Neurological treatment produced marked motor improvement and was associated with improvement in the foot phenomenon about which the patient had complained. From the patient's perspective, that could reinforce confidence in the specialists. Professionally, the same response potentially supplied contrary information.

The treatment reassured the patient while giving Neurology a reason to reconsider.

During procyclidine withdrawal, the patient contemporaneously reported increasing tension, restless feet and deterioration of his walk. In April 2018 the treating neurologist himself recorded that the first thing changing, and changing rapidly, was the return on the foot of the “outside left corn” phenomenon, with the patient becoming more “shifty” and “unrelaxed.”

This did not prove that a keratotic corn physically disappeared and reappeared pharmacologically. It created a neurological question: what changed at the interface between neurological motor state and the painful load-bearing foot?

Even if July 2017 “Not clear” was initially reasonable, did the 2018 dechallenge require the original neurological–foot relationship to be reopened? If not, why not?

The mechanical target

Podiatry repeatedly documented recurrent focal pathology at the left fifth MTPJ, including HD/callus, clawed toes, debridement, enucleation, direct offloading, insoles and toe props. That establishes real structural and mechanical pathology. It does not establish neurological independence.

Was the structural lesion mechanically independent, neurologically influenced, or mixed?

The boundary keeps being crossed

In 2019 primary care expressly linked the feet to increased tone and abnormal Parkinsonian gait. In 2022 the neurological record says the patient had bought a dog and was walking 2.5–3 hours daily, yet most mornings the feet took 20–25 minutes to “warm up” and remained sore around the corns and calluses.

By 2023 severe bilateral burning foot and lower-leg pain had generated urgent GP concern, “death wish” language and a Parkinson's-nurse report of debilitating pain and “no quality of life” despite pregabalin 600 mg/day. The later Neurology review genuinely reconsidered current mechanisms, including clawing, OFF dystonia, neuropathic possibilities, Parkinsonian non-motor pain fluctuation and levodopa state. Later that year severe pain plus shuffling was treated as OFF-related.

In 2024 pregabalin relieved much of the pain while activity-dependent toe curling persisted. In 2025 Neurology recorded that once Sinemet “hits his system” the feet feel better from a pain perspective and that more Sinemet would probably give further pain relief, although with dyskinesia risk.

Successive observations repeatedly acquired enough clinical significance to influence treatment, describe phenotype or explain symptoms, without clearly producing equivalent global revision of the original neurological–foot relationship.

Clinical lock-in

The leading systems hypothesis is therefore:

a founding synthesis creates a boundary → the boundary shapes the care pathway → later evidence is repeatedly used within that pathway without clearly being allowed to test and revise the boundary itself.

This is not itself proof of negligence, confirmation bias or motive. It is the structure to be tested by independent expert evidence.

The repository describes the record-level pattern as selective utilisation without integrative revision, or more compactly: local updating, global non-revision.

The patient remained inside the system he trusted

The tablets worked. He regained movement. He bought a dog. He walked for hours. Then the unresolved point where his neurological condition met the ground increasingly constrained the life that treatment had restored.

The clinicians did not have to foresee in July 2017 that foot pain would one day produce documented “death wish” language. The narrower question is whether reasonable neurological practice required them to understand, revisit and manage the relationship between an abnormal lower-limb motor disorder and the painful abnormal foot attached to it.

The ultimate question

How did a service sophisticated enough to recognise dystonia, recessive Parkinsonism and PRKN fail, if the expert evidence so finds, to take adequate ownership of the disabling feet through which that same abnormal movement was being lived?

The legal questions remain separate: what happened, including whether the Protest occurred; at what point reasonable neurological practice required something different; and what difference competent care would probably have made.
