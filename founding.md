# 1. The case in one paragraph

The patient entered Neurology in 2017 with painful feet, abnormal gait, pathological tone, toe-clawing and deteriorating function. Neurology found severe young-onset Parkinsonism, a dystonic-looking lower-limb abnormality and genuine structural foot disease, but left the relationship between the neurological disorder and the painful feet “Not clear.” Before discharge, the patient directly told the treating neurologist that the admission was incomplete because the feet had not been adequately returned to. The final discharge account omitted that Protest. Instead, it preserved material supporting separation, including that the patient had “in his own mind — markedly divorced” the foot pain from the leg tension, and it described him as intelligent, insightful, counselled, understanding and leaving with a clear plan. Later evidence repeatedly showed that the feet and lower limbs changed with neurological state and treatment, yet no identifiable overall reassessment of the original relationship appears. By 2025, Sinemet was known to improve the feet and the treating neurologist said this had been understood for some time. When the patient challenged the history, the neurologist relied on the historical letters as evidence of longstanding reasoning and shared understanding. The problem is therefore simple: **the hospital relied on a continuous history whose founding document had omitted the fact that the chosen course was expressly contested before it became final.**

# 2. What happened

1. The patient was referred with an integrated problem involving his feet, gait, tone, toe-clawing, movement and function.

2. Neurology diagnosed severe young-onset Parkinsonism and identified a lower-limb abnormality suggestive of dystonic posture, while also recording structural foot abnormalities.

3. The relationship between the neurological findings and the painful feet was not resolved. The final neurological account said the leg and foot symptoms might be separate or neuropathic but concluded: **“Not clear.”**

4. The patient's own observation that foot pain and leg tension were “not the same problem” was strengthened in the final account into the proposition that he had **“in his own mind — markedly divorced”** them.

5. Before discharge, the patient directly protested that the admission was incomplete because Neurology had not adequately returned to the painful feet.

6. The final synthesis omitted that Protest but preserved extensive statements that the patient was intelligent, insightful, well informed, counselled, understood the treatment strategy and was leaving with a clear plan.

7. Over the following years, neurological treatment and neurological deterioration repeatedly affected the feet and lower limbs. Primary care also repeatedly linked foot problems with Parkinsonian tone and abnormal gait.

8. In 2023, severe foot and leg pain was treated by Movement Disorders as OFF-related, and the record expressly acknowledged disagreement between the patient and neurologist about how the feet fitted into the Parkinsonian picture.

9. By March 2025, the neurologist recorded that the patient's feet improved when Sinemet took effect; controlled-release Sinemet then produced profound relief.

10. When the patient challenged the historical pathway, the 2025 response relied on the letters themselves to show that doctor and patient had shared a clear understanding for some time and quoted the 2017 “good understanding” statement in support of the historical treatment approach.

# 3. Why the hospital record cannot prove itself

The first problem is that the founding record is incomplete in a material way. It records the course the neurologist chose but omits the fact that, before that course became final, the patient directly challenged its adequacy. The record therefore preserves the decision but not the full decision process.

The second problem is that the omission changes the apparent position of the patient. The final account contains a patient who separates symptoms, understands treatment reasoning, receives counselling and leaves with a clear plan. It does not contain the same patient saying that the plan is incomplete. That makes the eventual disposition appear more settled and shared than the full encounter actually was.

The third problem is that later statements about what the patient knew or preferred cannot repair that founding omission. “Good understanding,” “fully counselled,” “well aware,” “reminded,” “if he wishes,” and later patient preferences may all be accurate statements about particular consultations. They do not prove that the patient accepted the adequacy of the original 2017 disposition.

The fourth problem is that the letters themselves were expressly used to create and preserve shared understanding. In 2019 the neurologist explained that the letters were written so that doctor and patient would have a “shared understanding of what I mean.” By 2025, those same letters were being cited as proof that a clear understanding had existed for some time.

That creates the central circularity. The founding letter omitted the patient's strongest contrary position; later care inherited the version that remained; later letters repeatedly recorded understanding and preference within that inherited framework; and those letters were then relied on to prove the historical adequacy of the framework itself.

# 4. Bottom line

This is not a case that requires the whole medical record to be false. Parkinsonism was real. Structural foot disease was real. Many individual treatment decisions may have been reasonable.

The defect is narrower and more serious.

The hospital's longitudinal account is not reliable as a complete and self-sufficient history of the material decision pathway because the first durable account omitted the patient's direct challenge to the adequacy of the plan, preserved instead a representation of the patient as understanding and participating in that plan, and then became the starting point from which later events, preferences and treatment decisions were interpreted.

The later record may prove that treatment occurred, that risks were discussed and that the patient made later choices. It cannot retrospectively prove that the original disposition was uncontested, complete or properly reconsidered merely because later documents remained consistent with it.

**The hospital cannot rely on the continuity of its own record as proof that the material decision pathway was complete and properly updated because that continuity begins with a founding record that omitted the very fact showing the pathway was contested before it became final, and the later record largely carries forward and relies upon that incomplete starting point.**
