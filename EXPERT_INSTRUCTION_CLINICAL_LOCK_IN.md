## Purpose of instruction

The central issue for expert determination is not whether every aspect of the claimant’s foot pathology was caused directly by Parkinsonism. The issue is whether Neurology, having identified a markedly abnormal lower-limb motor disorder and having considered very-young-onset/autosomal-recessive Parkinsonism including PRKN/PARK2, adequately investigated, formulated and subsequently managed the relationship between that motor disorder and the claimant’s painful abnormal feet.

The expert is asked to distinguish:

1. reasonable diagnostic uncertainty;
2. inadequate investigation producing apparent uncertainty;
3. reasonable separation of structural and neurological pathology;
4. premature or unsupported clinical partitioning of the feet from the neurological disorder;
5. failure to revise that partition when later evidence emerged.

## 1. The original neurological task

The claimant was referred with an integrated presentation involving abnormal gait, painful abnormal feet, increased muscle tone and progressive neurological dysfunction.

During the July 2017 admission Neurology documented a “strikingly abnormal” gait and a lower-limb appearance “somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture – NOT FOOT DROP.”

The DAT-scan request subsequently stated:

> “Walk is almost dystonically unusual. We suspect an Autosomal recessive ‘Idiopathic’, Parkinsons.”

The discharge synthesis separately identified clinical features supporting possible PRKN/PARK2: very young age of onset, akinetic rigidity, absence of dominant family history, marked symmetry and excellent cognition.

The expert should address whether, in that clinical context, reasonable neurological practice required specific consideration of whether the abnormal painful feet, toe deformity/clawing, gait disturbance and loading pattern were manifestations of, consequences of, or materially influenced by the lower-limb movement disorder.

Particular attention should be paid to the fact that the dystonic lower-limb feature was present in the examination and was expressly linked to suspected autosomal-recessive Parkinsonism in the DAT request, but was not included among the features later enumerated in the PRKN/PARK2 phenotype.

The expert should consider whether this represents a clinically significant failure of phenotypic integration.

## 2. The “Not clear” conclusion

The discharge synthesis states that the leg symptoms including corns, calluses and burning may have been “completely separate,” perhaps secondary to peripheral neuropathy, and concludes “Not clear.”

The claimant does not contend that uncertainty itself constituted negligence.

The question is whether adequate neurological work had been performed before that uncertainty was operationalised.

The expert is asked to identify, from the contemporaneous record, what examination, investigation or reasoning demonstrates that the relationship between the neurological lower-limb disorder and the painful feet had been adequately assessed.

If that foundation cannot be identified, the expert should address whether “Not clear” represented:

- residual uncertainty after adequate assessment; or
- uncertainty resulting from failure adequately to investigate the relationship.

That distinction is central.

## 3. Clinical partition and disposition

The subsequent management placed the feet into a Podiatry pathway.

The expert should consider whether Podiatry referral was appropriate for structural pathology but insufficient as the neurological response to an unresolved relationship between dystonia/tone/gait and foot pain.

The relevant question is not whether Podiatry was appropriate.

It is whether Neurology had completed its own task before transferring practical foot care elsewhere.

The expert should identify whether the referral contained any defined neurological question concerning dystonia, abnormal loading, posture, increased tone or gait, and whether there was any mechanism by which Podiatry findings were intended to return to Neurology for integrated interpretation.

## 4. The alleged pre-discharge Protest

The claimant’s account is that immediately before discharge he expressly complained that the principal disabling foot problem had not been dealt with.

That factual allegation requires separate proof and should not be assumed by the expert as established.

However, the expert is asked to address the following hypothetical:

If that account is accepted, what would reasonable neurological practice have required before maintaining discharge?

Specifically, would a reasonably competent neurologist have been expected to:

- revisit the assessment;
- examine the neurological relationship of the feet to the abnormal gait;
- obtain movement-disorders input;
- explain why no further neurological assessment was required;
- or arrange defined follow-up of the unresolved relationship?

The expert should also address whether such a challenge and the clinical decision made in response would ordinarily constitute material information for the discharge record and subsequent GP.

## 5. The 2018 procyclidine dechallenge

This is a separate and potentially independent breach issue.

In April 2018 the treating neurologist recorded that during a two-week withdrawal of procyclidine:

> “the first thing he noticed changing and changing rapidly was the return on the foot, of the outside left corn.”

The note also records worsening symptoms and the claimant being more “shifty” and “unrelaxed.”

The expert is not asked to assume that a structural corn itself disappeared and reappeared pharmacologically.

Rather, the expert should address what clinical significance should reasonably have been attached to a focal foot phenomenon changing rapidly with neurological treatment state.

Possible mechanisms may include altered dystonia, posture, tone, gait, loading, pain generation or pain modulation.

The central question is:

> **Even if the July 2017 uncertainty was initially reasonable, did the 2018 dechallenge require the original foot/neurological relationship to be reopened?**

If not, the expert should explain why not.

## 6. Recurrent structural pathology

Podiatry repeatedly documented focal pathology at the left fifth MTPJ, including callus/HD, debridement, enucleation, clawed toes and mechanical offloading.

The expert should consider whether structural pathology excludes neurological involvement.

The claimant’s case is not that structural pathology did not exist.

The question is whether repeated pathology at a load-bearing site could reasonably have been mechanically influenced by abnormal neurological posture or gait.

The expert should therefore distinguish:

> **structural pathology**

from

> **structurally independent pathology**.

Those are not equivalent.

## 7. Evidence of later integration without reconstruction

The later record repeatedly contains observations crossing the original neurological/foot boundary.

In 2019 the GP described the foot problems as related to increased tone and abnormal gait associated with Parkinson’s disease.

In 2022 the claimant was walking 2.5–3 hours daily, but his feet took 20–25 minutes to “warm up” in the morning and remained sore around the corns and calluses.

By 2023 Neurology was considering clawing, OFF dystonia and Parkinsonian non-motor pain fluctuation. Later in 2023 severe foot pain together with shuffling was expressly considered OFF-related and treatment was altered with Stalevo.

By 2024 standing activities worsened foot pain, toe curling occurred with exertion, and pregabalin relieved pain without abolishing the motor toe-curling phenomenon.

By 2025 Neurology recorded that once Sinemet “hits his system” the feet feel better from a pain perspective and accepted that increasing Sinemet would probably provide additional pain relief, although at the risk of dyskinesia.

The expert should consider whether these later observations demonstrate genuine reconstruction of the original foot formulation, or whether they represent treatment and explanation occurring within a framework that continued to preserve the original clinical separation.

## 8. Psychological distress and safeguarding

By March 2023 the GP made an urgent neurological referral describing severe bilateral burning pain in the feet and lower legs, “extreme at time and causing death wish,” stating:

> “I am worried about him”

and requesting review:

> “before we get into trouble here.”

The Parkinson’s nurse separately recorded debilitating pain and “no quality of life” despite pregabalin 600 mg/day.

The expert should distinguish psychological distress caused by severe persistent pain from a primary psychiatric explanation for the pain.

The relevant questions are:

- whether the severity of distress required documented psychological-risk assessment or safeguarding;
- whether the existence of such distress increased the urgency of neurological reassessment;
- and whether reasonable care required integrated consideration of pain, gait, OFF state, dystonia and functional deterioration.

The claimant does not contend that Neurology in 2017 was required to foresee suicidality years later.

The foreseeability question is narrower.

Was it reasonably foreseeable that failure adequately to identify and manage an abnormal motor–foot relationship could permit persistent abnormal loading, recurrent foot pathology, pain and progressive functional consequences?

## 9. Breach propositions

The expert is ultimately asked to state whether, on the balance of professional opinion, any of the following constituted care below the standard of a reasonably competent neurologist:

1. failure adequately to investigate the feet–gait–tone relationship in July 2017;
2. premature separation of the feet from the neurological formulation;
3. failure, assuming the Protest occurred, to reconsider after express notice before discharge;
4. inadequate neurological handoff to Podiatry;
5. failure to reopen the original formulation after the 2018 procyclidine dechallenge;
6. failure to integrate recurrent focal foot pathology with motor-state evidence;
7. failure adequately to reassess when severe foot pain later became associated with major psychological distress.

For each alleged breach, the expert should identify:

- the earliest date at which breach occurred;
- what reasonable practice required;
- what was omitted;
- and why that omission fell below acceptable neurological practice.

## 10. Causation

Breach and causation should be addressed separately.

The claimant must ultimately establish not merely that further assessment should have occurred, but what would probably have followed.

The expert should therefore identify the likely counterfactual pathway if competent integrated neurological assessment had occurred.

This may include, if clinically justified:

- movement-disorders assessment;
- explicit assessment of lower-limb dystonia;
- analysis of pathological loading;
- coordinated Neurology–Podiatry management;
- treatment-state testing;
- revised pharmacological strategy;
- earlier therapeutic intervention;
- or other appropriate measures.

The expert should distinguish what was merely possible from what was probable.

The ultimate causation question is:

> **Had the abnormal foot been maintained within, or subsequently restored to, the neurological formulation, is it more likely than not that the duration or severity of pain, structural injury, functional limitation or psychological distress would have been materially reduced?**

## Ultimate issue

The case should not be reduced to whether all foot pain was “neurological.”

The ultimate issue is whether Neurology created and maintained a clinically unsupported boundary between the neurological motor disorder and the painful load-bearing foot, despite repeatedly encountering evidence capable of challenging that boundary.

The critical expert question is therefore:

> **Why did evidence repeatedly cross the neurological-foot boundary without the boundary itself being revised?**

If reasonable neurological practice required that revision, the failure to perform it is the central alleged breach.
