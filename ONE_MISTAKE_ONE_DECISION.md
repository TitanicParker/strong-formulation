# One Mistake, One Protest, One Decision

**Status:** CONTROLLING SIMPLIFICATION — 8 September 2026

The central 2017 case can be stated without carrying the whole later analytical apparatus in the first sentence.

> **One mistake → the Protest identifies it → one decision → omission → consequences.**

## 1. The mistake

The GP referred one integrated clinical problem: painful abnormal feet and gait, with pathological lower-limb tone, toe-clawing, movement difficulty and deteriorating function explaining why Neurology was required.

Neurology identified severe very-young-onset Parkinsonism and developed that part of the case extensively. The painful abnormal feet through which the referral began were not equivalently reconstructed after those neurological discoveries.

The mistake alleged by the project is therefore not the Parkinson's diagnosis.

It is:

> **the failure to complete the neurological construction of the presenting feet–gait–tone problem before closure.**

That remains an allegation requiring expert assessment as to clinical standard.

## 2. The Protest identifies the mistake while the decision is still live

Shortly before discharge, the patient says in substance:

> **You have not properly dealt with my feet. You cannot be finished.**

The Protest does not present a new diagnosis. It identifies the incompleteness directly, before the admission has closed.

This is why the Protest is the hinge.

> **Before the Protest, the problem can be described as incomplete construction. After the Protest, closure becomes a decision made with the incompleteness expressly raised.**

## 3. One decision

The answer is **Podiatry**.

Source-level investigation now shows that Podiatry was already an active GP-origin pathway. The presently identified patient-facing record does not show a distinct post-Protest neurological handover question or return mechanism that converted that existing pathway into an investigation of the feet–tone–gait relationship.

The decisive act is therefore not referral to Podiatry in the abstract.

It is:

> **the decision to proceed to neurological closure rather than reopen the integrated problem the patient had just restored.**

## 4. The synthesis makes that decision portable

The later-finalised discharge synthesis contains the relevant facts but allocates them directionally: Parkinsonism/PRKN/treatment strategy are extensively developed; the painful feet can be separate, perhaps neuropathic, “Not clear,” and operationally Podiatry.

The patient’s limited phenomenological distinction is strengthened into **“markedly divorced.”**

The Protest does not travel.

Thus:

> **The synthesis turns the closure decision into the durable information state later clinicians inherit.**

## 5. The omission

The omitted fact is not merely that the patient disagreed.

It is that, before closure, the patient expressly challenged the adequacy of the process generating the conclusion.

The omission therefore changes the represented decision history.

Without the Protest, later readers see:

> **specialist assessment → uncertainty → Podiatry → completed discharge.**

With the Protest, the sequence is:

> **specialist assessment → patient says the originating problem remains unfinished → decision not to reopen → Podiatry / closure.**

Those are materially different information states.

## 6. Consequences

The later record matters because it shows what followed from that durable starting state.

Podiatry developed focal fifth-MTPJ mechanical information. Neurology developed dynamic treatment-state information. Primary care later regenerated an integrated feet–tone–gait formulation. Later Neurology genuinely reconsidered the current problem and developed a mixed model.

Those later facts do not need to be front-loaded into the liability theory.

Their primary functions are:

- to show that the unresolved relationship remained clinically meaningful;
- to test the durability of the founding allocation;
- to trace documentary and clinical consequences;
- and to ask whether the omitted fork was ever historically reconstructed.

## 7. What 2023 is—and is not

The 2023 pain/mobility/falls/psychological-safety episode may present a separate contemporaneous clinical question.

It should **not** complicate the controlling 2017 proposition.

The repository therefore treats it separately:

> **2017 asks whether one identified incompleteness was knowingly closed without reopening and then omitted from the portable account.**

> **2023 asks whether a later pain–mobility–falls–psychological crisis was adequately integrated and managed as a present safety problem.**

They may reveal similar information-arrangement behaviour, but they are not the same alleged breach and should not be fused.

## 8. The shortest case

> **The mistake was an incomplete neurological construction of the problem the GP sent in. The patient identified that mistake before discharge. The clinician then had to decide whether to reopen it or close. He closed, using Podiatry as the destination. The final synthesis made that decision portable and omitted the Protest that had exposed the incompleteness. The later history is the consequence and test of that founding decision, not the centre of the liability theory.**

Or shorter still:

> **One mistake. One Protest. One decision. One omission. Then the consequences.**

## 9. Discipline

This simplification does not itself prove:

- breach of duty;
- the correct foot mechanism;
- that every later consequence was caused by the 2017 decision;
- deliberate concealment;
- or that 2023 was negligent.

Its purpose is structural: keep the central alleged 2017 error distinct from the later evidence used to understand its consequences and from any separate later safety question.
