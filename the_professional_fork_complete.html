A medical narrative about a professional decision

The Professional Fork

A natural history of one professional decision: how a patient’s Protest at the end of an eight-day University Hospital admission exposed an unfinished neurological problem, how specialist uncertainty acquired the force to organise downstream care, and how the body spent eight years returning to a question the record had already learned how to survive.

Complete edition · red quotations are documentary or patient words · narrative synthesis without footnotes

There is a moment in this story when the medicine and the profession part company in the surviving record. Before it, the patient is still an unfinished clinical problem. After it, the unresolved medical question is converted into something the professional system can carry forward without answering. The moment is not a laboratory result. It is not the DaTscan. It is not the later genetic confirmation. It is the patient’s Protest, close to discharge, when he tells Neurology that the feet have not been properly investigated and therefore Neurology cannot yet be finished.

This essay follows that fork. The backstage conversation need not be invented because the observable sequence is strong enough. An obvious incompleteness is exposed in front of a teaching audience. The department then produces a sophisticated formulation involving dystonia, very-young-onset Parkinsonism and possible PRKN/PARK2. Yet the feet are not visibly reopened as a movement-disorders problem before the pathway closes. The question is therefore exact: was the new understanding used to reopen the medicine — or to make the professional problem closable?

1. Watch the case before the fork

Begin before the hospital, because scale matters. This was not a patient arriving with three days of odd symptoms and asking a consultant to divine a rare diagnosis from mist. There had been roughly thirteen years of bodily history by the summer of 2017. The feet had hurt for years. The patient’s description was not decorative: My feet were fucking killing me. He described them as scorching hot and filled with burning pain. Both of them. He had worked standing behind a bar, sought chiropody, lived through recurrent corns and calluses, and learned by brute experience how pain on one part of the foot could alter posture and loading elsewhere.

Alongside the feet came another phenomenon: tension. Legs that would not relax. A body that felt increasingly wooden. Difficulty initiating movement. Progressive stiffness. The patient distinguished the sensations. Foot pain was foot pain; leg tension was leg tension. This distinction is perfectly ordinary. People can distinguish burning from stiffness, pressure from pulling, a corn from a muscle that will not release. What a patient cannot do from sensation alone is decide whether two experiences arise from unrelated diseases.

By 2017 the GP had begun to see the whole animal rather than its separate tracks. The primary-care record moves from bilateral foot pain and abnormal gait to mallet toes, calluses, pathological tone, brisk reflexes and difficulty initiating movement. The GP specifically contemplated Neurology for gait, feet and “muscle tension” issues. The urgent referral did not send two patients to hospital. It sent one: painful abnormal feet, abnormal gait, pathological tone, deteriorating function.

THE REFERRAL QUESTION WAS INTEGRATED BEFORE NEUROLOGY EVER TOUCHED IT.

On 3 July 2017 that patient entered University Hospital Galway. He remained for eight days. This matters because eight inpatient days in a University Hospital are not a fleeting encounter. There was time to observe. Time to repeat an examination. Time to ask what came first. Time to compare painful structure with movement. Time to notice what a foot did when the patient walked, stood, rested, turned and tried to initiate movement. Time, above all, to preserve uncertainty where uncertainty remained.

2. Neurology finds something real — and large

The neurological work was not empty. Quite the opposite. Severe very-young-onset Parkinsonism was recognised. There was marked symmetrical rigidity, gross bradykinesia and a strikingly abnormal gait. Tremor, importantly, was not the dominant feature. The record repeatedly emphasises its relative absence: very little tremor; later, lack of prominent tremor. The case therefore announced itself principally through slowness, stiffness, gait, posture and motor control.

The finished 2017 account also contains a lower-limb observation that will matter for years: the gait had a foot-drop appearance, but was described as somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture – NOT FOOT DROP. That is sophisticated movement description. But its timing must remain honest. We do not know from the surviving final document whether those words existed before the Protest, after it, or were refined during post-discharge transcription. The uncertainty matters because the document itself was not simply a bedside diary. The patient was discharged on 11 July; the record contains a transcription-finalisation addendum dated 13 July at 21:41.

So the final letter is not a transparent window onto a single moment of thought. It is a constructed object: examination findings, test results, differential diagnosis, counselling, genetic speculation, treatment strategy and later addendum brought together into one durable narrative.

That narrative became highly sophisticated. Very young age. Symmetry. Akinetic-rigid phenotype. Atypical gait. Genetic Parkinsonism. PRKN/PARK2. Levodopa sensitivity. A cautious treatment strategy. It became, in short, the kind of document that can look retrospectively as though the whole case had been conceptually mastered.

But one part of the original problem remained primitive. The feet hurt. They had hurt for years. The feet had structure, pressure, calluses, clawing and an abnormal relationship with gait. And as discharge approached, the patient realised that Neurology had not answered the question he had actually brought with him.

3. The Protest

There is a difference between a complaint made months later and an objection made while a clinical decision is still live. According to the patient, this objection occurred before discharge, in front of four medical students, with the department’s hierarchy and teaching environment around the encounter. The substance was simple: the feet had not been properly dealt with; therefore Neurology could not be finished.

That is not the same as asking for a corn to be pared. The patient already knew Podiatry. He had lived with local foot treatment for years. He was saying that the neurological investigation of the presentation was incomplete.

The remembered answer was one word:

Podiatry.

At that point two possible futures become visible. They are not abstract ethical theories. They are practical clinical pathways.

Path A — reopen the medicine

Stop discharge long enough to revisit the feet as part of the movement disorder. Examine posture, power, dystonia, loading and pain again. Ask whether the structural lesion and neurological state interact. Bring in movement-disorders expertise if the consultant recognises a limitation. Preserve the unresolved relationship explicitly in the plan. Let the patient’s objection change what happens next.

Path B — close the professional problem

Keep the discharge destination intact. Let Podiatry own the painful feet. Let Neurology own Parkinsonism. Preserve uncertainty in prose — Not clear — but do not permit that uncertainty to delay closure. Complete a sophisticated explanatory document capable of carrying the decision forward.

The argument of this essay is that the subsequent record increasingly resembles Path B.

That is a serious claim. It should therefore be stated precisely. We do not know every private conversation after the Protest. We do not know when each line of the final note was first drafted. We cannot assign NOT FOOT DROP to before or after the Protest. What we can observe is the asymmetry between what the finished document came to know and what the clinical pathway did not visibly reopen.

4. The authority of “Not clear”

Two words in the finalised 2017 correspondence carry far more weight than they first appear to carry:

Not clear.

Read as ordinary English, this looks modest. The neurologist is not pretending to know what he does not know. The painful feet, corns, calluses and burning may be completely separate, perhaps related to a peripheral neuropathy; the relationship is Not clear. There is nothing inherently improper about diagnostic uncertainty. Medicine is full of it.

But uncertainty never arrives without a speaker. The same words have radically different force depending on who says them, where they are said, and what the words are allowed to do next. If a stranger says the relationship is not clear, that statement has no clinical jurisdiction. If a monarch says it, the words may carry social prestige but no medical authority. If the GP says it, the uncertainty has clinical meaning — and in this case the GP’s uncertainty is one of the reasons the patient is sent upward to specialist care in the first place.

Then the direction of travel reverses.

The GP has seen painful abnormal feet, abnormal gait, clawing, pathological tone, brisk reflexes and deteriorating function. Primary care cannot satisfactorily explain the relationship, so the patient is referred into University Hospital Neurology. The unresolved relation is not the end of the GP’s work. It is the reason for specialist investigation.

Eight inpatient days later, after severe very-young-onset Parkinsonism has been identified, after the patient has protested that the feet remain neurologically unfinished, and inside a consultant letter returning the case to the GP, Not clear acquires a wholly different character.

BEFORE REFERRAL, “NOT CLEAR” WAS A REASON TO INVESTIGATE. AFTER THE CONSULTANT’S LETTER, “NOT CLEAR” BECAME SOMETHING THE SYSTEM COULD ACT UPON.

This is specialist-certified uncertainty at the point of disposition.

The consultant is not one observer among many. He is the specialist to whom the unresolved problem has travelled for clarification. He is also the professional closing the neurological admission and writing the durable account by which the GP, Podiatry, future Neurology staff and later governance will understand what happened. His uncertainty therefore has authority, direction and portability.

And it does not travel alone. It travels beside a sophisticated formulation: severe Parkinsonism; a foot-drop-like gait somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture – NOT FOOT DROP; genetic Parkinsonism in play; possible PRKN/PARK2; structural foot disease; possible neuropathy; the suggestion that the painful feet may have been completely separate; the statement that the patient had in his own mind – markedly divorced the leg tension from the foot pain; and the operational answer of Podiatry.

So Not clear does not mean: we do not know, therefore the relationship remains clinically active and requires a defined method of resolution. In the architecture of the completed letter it can function more like: we do not know, but we know enough to proceed.

That is its peculiar power.

The question has not been answered. Its unanswered state has been professionalised.

This is not a semantic trick. It affects downstream information states directly. The GP receiving the letter is not receiving the original untouched puzzle back. He is receiving the puzzle after an eight-day Neurology admission, accompanied by the specialist’s account of what the uncertainty means and where the feet now belong. A future clinician is not starting from the raw referral. A future clinician starts downstream of an authoritative synthesis.

This is why the wording Not clear may ultimately matter more than a simple wrong answer. A categorical diagnosis can later be disproved. Authoritative uncertainty is more durable. It sounds careful. It does not expose itself easily to falsification. Yet if no exit condition is attached to it, it can organise care for years.

The missing exit condition

What would make the relationship clear enough to reopen? The letter does not tell us. There is no visible rule saying: if neurological medication changes the feet, revisit the partition. If the same outside-left-foot site changes rapidly with neurological state, revisit it. If Podiatry repeatedly treats the same mechanical consequence, reconsider what is loading the site. If OFF periods alter pain or gait, reconstruct the relation. If dystonia later becomes explicit, reopen. If levodopa repeatedly changes the feet, reopen.

Without such an exit condition, uncertainty can become an organising permission. Almost anything can be added later without forcing the original arrangement to collapse.

A corn can remain mechanical. A foot throw can later become actually parkinsonian. Pain can become OFF-related. Dystonia can become plausible. Pregabalin can help temporarily and fail later. Stalevo can be taken for pain. Controlled-release Sinemet can produce profound relief. Each fact can be dealt with locally while the founding relationship remains Not clear.

That is what makes this small phrase so consequential. It does not erase the feet. It determines the professional conditions under which the unresolved feet are allowed to continue existing.

Then there is the second phrase:

in his own mind – markedly divorced

The patient did distinguish foot pain from leg tension. He knew burning from stiffness, pressure from an internal drive to tense. That is phenomenology. But markedly is the clinician’s intensifier, and divorced gives the distinction causal force. The phrase can therefore move from the patient experiences these sensations differently to the patient himself regarded them as separate problems.

Yet the patient’s Protest points in the opposite direction. At the moment closure becomes live, he is saying that whatever differences he feels between pain and tension, the feet remain part of the unfinished neurological question.

The durable document preserves the clinician-amplified separation. It does not preserve the Protest.

That asymmetry is not background detail. It determines which version of uncertainty acquires authority.

5. After notice, continuation becomes a choice

Before the Protest, the emerging partition can still be described as a clinical plan in motion. Neurology has found severe Parkinsonism. Podiatry exists for local foot disease. The team is building a treatment strategy. It is possible, before the objection, to say that the case is simply evolving through ordinary hospital processes.

Then the patient objects before discharge.

That changes the ethical status of everything that follows.

He does not need to know the word PRKN. He does not need to identify dystonia. He does not need to understand dopamine pharmacology. He does not need to tell a consultant how to practise Neurology. He needs only to identify the failure that is visible from the one perspective nobody else possesses: the thing that brought me here is still not dealt with.

The Protest therefore puts the plan on trial while the plan is still changeable.

Once that happens, continuing exactly as before is no longer merely continuation. It is continuation after notice.

BEFORE THE PROTEST, THE PARTITION CAN BE A PLAN. AT THE PROTEST, THE PLAN IS CHALLENGED. AFTER THE PROTEST, CONTINUING IT BECOMES A CHOICE.

This is why the omission of the Protest from the durable record matters so much. Without it, the later reader sees an apparently natural sequence: investigation, uncertainty, plan, discharge. Restore the objection and the sequence changes: investigation, unresolved originating problem, proposed disposition, patient explicitly challenges the sufficiency of that disposition, alternative clinical action remains available, existing course is nevertheless continued, discharge proceeds.

The omitted event is therefore not merely a missing complaint. It is evidence that another path remained live.

And the clinical alternative was remarkably small.

Reopen the feet. Examine them neurologically in light of what has now been discovered. Map posture, power, dystonic drive, loading and pain. Ask whether the structural lesion is being produced or aggravated by abnormal motor behaviour. Decide what Podiatry is meant to answer and what it cannot answer. If the consultant recognises that the problem lies outside his movement-disorders expertise, make that recognition operational: shared care, referral, transfer, departmental escalation. If discharge still proceeds, state explicitly what remains unresolved and what future observation will trigger reconsideration.

None of this requires a dramatic therapeutic gamble. It does not require aggressive levodopa. It does not require deciding in July 2017 that every painful corn is dystonia. It requires permitting the patient’s objection to change the clinical state of play.

The medical reopening is small.

The professional reversal is larger.

Because reopening means acknowledging that after eight days of sophisticated University Hospital Neurology, the originating problem remains insufficiently integrated at the point of discharge. In a teaching environment, after the incompleteness has been exposed in front of students, that is not merely another clinical datum. It is a professional problem too.

This is where the fork becomes ethically interesting. The department does not have to choose between medicine and administration in some cartoonish sense. It has to decide whether the administrative and professional momentum already accumulated around discharge will yield when the patient identifies a still-live medical question.

The record that follows appears to solve the tension by allowing both states to coexist. The letter becomes more intellectually sophisticated. Uncertainty is acknowledged. The feet receive a destination. The discharge survives.

The file records incompleteness and performs completion.

That is more consequential than an ordinary diagnostic error. An ordinary error can remain provisional and later be corrected. Here, the unresolved relationship is converted into an operationally stable arrangement.

And because the Protest itself does not travel forward in the durable synthesis, later readers inherit the verdict without inheriting the fact that the verdict had been contested while another course was still available.

6. The first synthesis gets a head start

Clinical records do not merely store facts. They determine the order in which future professionals encounter them.

A later clinician does not walk into July 2017 with the patient and watch the week unfold. The later clinician meets the finished synthesis. Parkinsonism is prominent, administratively named and later reinforced by an abnormal DaTscan. The feet remain present, but they enter through the interpretive channels already created for them: structural pathology, possible neuropathy, Podiatry, patient-described separation, Not clear.

This gives the founding synthesis narrative priority.

Once a formulation has that head start, later information does not arrive on neutral ground. It arrives as an amendment to a story that already possesses professional authority. The later fact must therefore do more than exist. It must become strong enough to force reconstruction of the story that receives it.

This is where a case can become progressively difficult to reverse even while nobody consciously decides that it is irreversible.

Diagnosis leads to discharge. Discharge leads to correspondence. Correspondence leads to follow-up. Follow-up presumes the correspondence. Treatments are explained through the diagnosis. Podiatry acts within its own remit. Later notes inherit prior notes. Eventually a complaint is answered by quoting the historical letters. Governance then reads those same letters as evidence that the historical reasoning was coherent.

Each layer raises the professional cost of returning to first principles.

The original question does not necessarily become better answered. It becomes more deeply buried beneath consequences generated by the first answer.

This is progressive documentary irreversibility: not because records literally cannot be changed, but because each downstream action makes the founding architecture more expensive to disturb.

The later years therefore matter for one reason above all. They allow us to watch whether biology can still make the record change its mind.

7. Genetics is not movement-disorders expertise

After the admission, Monaghan did something important and appropriate: he sought genetic neurological expertise. He wrote to Professor Daniel Healy asking what genetic testing would be appropriate, whether DNA could be analysed, and whether the exercise would help. University Hospital Galway would pay if necessary. A once-off opinion in Dublin was contemplated. Healy later wrote that the patient probably has Park II, recommended PARK2 testing first and a broader panel if negative, and recorded dramatic response to procyclidine and rasagiline.

This matters because it demonstrates that expertise was available and that the PRKN hypothesis was not a stray thought buried at the bottom of a differential. It became serious enough for external genetic advice. In 2018 the treating neurologist himself still wrote of the specific PARK 2 PRKN which we/Prof Healy suspect is the cause.

And the local expertise was not theoretical. On 15 August 2017, weeks after this admission, neurologists from the same Galway department — Patrick Cullinane, Patrick Browne, Michael Hennessy and Timothy Counihan — published a clinical-reasoning case of a young patient whose abnormal gait included a dystonic foot posture resembling foot drop and whose diagnosis proved to be juvenile Parkinsonism caused by Parkin mutations. The point is not that another patient’s case supplies this patient’s diagnosis. The point is institutional capacity. At the precise time this patient’s post-Protest correspondence was acquiring PRKN and dystonia sophistication, the department itself contained contemporary expertise in the combination of youth, gait, foot posture, dystonia, Parkinsonism and PRKN.

But genetics is not the same professional problem as movement phenomenology. Healy’s role cannot be used retrospectively as the answer to a different sentence written by Monaghan:

I am not a movement disorders neurologist.

That statement occurs inside his own reasoning about a very-young-onset, genetically suspicious, treatment-sensitive movement disorder. It is an admission of a subspecialty limit in the very territory he is managing. The relevant question is not whether he had ever consulted another neurologist for something. The question is what operational consequence followed this particular limitation.

Was there a movement-disorders referral? Shared ownership? Transfer? Departmental escalation? A specialist reassessment of the unresolved dystonic feet? The present record has not shown one.

That is particularly striking because Monaghan had already demonstrated the capacity to seek expertise when he believed a question required it. He sought genetic expertise for genetics. The issue is why the movement-disorders limitation appears as a documentary disclaimer rather than an equally visible transfer of the movement-disorders problem.

8. The years become an evidential corridor

From this point onward, chronology is useful only if it asks the same question repeatedly.

Does each new observation reconstruct the founding relationship, or is it accommodated inside the arrangement created after the Protest?

That distinction prevents the middle of this story from becoming a list of clinic visits. There are at least three ways later medicine can respond to new information.

Independent reconstruction: return to the beginning, reopen the original feet–gait–dystonia–Parkinsonism relationship, and ask whether the founding partition still makes sense.

Inherited continuation: proceed within the established model because that is the model the record already supplies.

Local accommodation: recognise that one aspect is neurological, mechanical, OFF-related, dystonic or medication-sensitive, and adjust something locally without reconsidering the governing relationship.

The later record contains many local accommodations. That is precisely why the case cannot honestly be described as the feet simply being ignored. But it would be equally misleading to say, without qualification, that “the feet were examined again and again.” For roughly eighteen months after discharge, it was Podiatry that repeatedly put hands on the feet. Neurology repeatedly discussed, classified and reinterpreted information about them. Those are different activities, performed in different professional compartments.

The harder question is whether they were ever allowed to regain governing power.

THE FEET WERE NOT EXCLUDED FROM THE RECORD. THE QUESTION IS WHETHER THEY WERE EXCLUDED FROM GOVERNING POWER.

The evidential corridor begins with a remarkably specific tracer: the outside of the left foot.

9. The outside of the left foot becomes a tracer

After discharge, Podiatry did competent and remarkably persistent local work. The disclosed treatment record runs from 14 September 2017 to 25 March 2019. There are eight treatment encounters. Eight out of eight document the same left lateral forefoot problem at the fifth metatarsophalangeal joint — the joint at the base of the little toe on the outside of the forefoot. The terminology varies between callus, hard corn and hard lesion, but the anatomical address does not.

On the first visit the callus is removed from the left fifth MTPJ. In October the hard corn at the same site is enucleated deeply enough to cause pin-prick bleeding and is then specifically offloaded. In January 2018 the hard lesion is again enucleated at the same site and again bleeding is produced. February still finds callus there. March still finds callus there, now with clawed toes. June still finds it there with bilateral clawing. In December the patient says, I get pain under the left side of my left foot; Podiatry again finds the left fifth-MTPJ callus, draws that exact site on a foot sketch and prescribes a cut-out to offload it. On 25 March 2019 the final disclosed treatment note again records callus overlying a hard lesion at the same left fifth MTPJ and enucleates it.

This is not eight examinations discovering eight unrelated things. It is eight encounters repeatedly finding and treating essentially the same mechanical endpoint in the same place. Tight calf musculature, clawed toes, pronation and altered loading are also documented. The mechanical pathology was real. Podiatry kept doing what Podiatry could do to it: remove it, protect it, redistribute pressure, modify footwear and build orthoses.

And that is precisely why the case becomes so revealing. Mechanical pathology and neurological pathology are not mutually exclusive. A neurologically altered foot can still grow a corn. A corn can still hurt. Abnormal posture, dystonic drive or altered loading can repeatedly recreate a mechanical lesion. Removing the lesion can reduce local damage without answering why the same pressure point keeps being generated.

Then Neurology acquires a different piece of information about the same territory. In April 2018 the neurologist records what had happened when procyclidine was stopped because of blurred vision:

the first thing he noticed changing and changing rapidly was the return on the foot, of the outside left corn.

The wording should not be abused. It does not prove that a mound of keratin regenerated at high speed. What it does establish is that the outside-left-foot phenomenon — at the precise territory Podiatry was repeatedly treating — changed rapidly with neurological drug state.

Now imagine the two services exchanging only their own half of the observation. If Neurology tells Podiatry, the outside-left-foot phenomenon changes when an anticholinergic is withdrawn, that fact does not by itself tell a podiatrist what neurological mechanism is operating. If Podiatry tells Neurology, we have removed and offloaded the same hard lesion at the same left fifth MTPJ again and again, twice deeply enough to produce bleeding, and the site still returns, that fact does not by itself tell a neurologist whether the driver is dystonia, rigidity, altered gait, medication state, sensory amplification or some combination.

The clinically important information existed in the join. Podiatry knew, with extraordinary anatomical consistency, where the mechanical consequence kept appearing. Neurology knew that the behaviour of that same outside-left-foot territory could change with neurological treatment state. The question requiring ownership was not whether the corn was real. It was what kept making that exact site behave this way in a patient whose lower-limb posture and gait were already neurologically abnormal.

No surviving record has been found showing that Neurology gave Podiatry the procyclidine-withdrawal observation. No surviving record shows Podiatry’s eight repeated same-site findings being brought back into Neurology and used to reconstruct the original feet–dystonia–Parkinsonism relationship. The patient moved between the two information states. The information did not.

PODIATRY KNEW WHERE IT WAS. NEUROLOGY KNEW IT COULD CHANGE WITH NEUROLOGICAL STATE. THE MISSING CLINICAL ACT WAS TO JOIN THOSE FACTS.

And then the Podiatry documentary trail stops. The patient record gives 25 March 2019 as the last visit, and the disclosed treatment notes contain nothing later. The record does not tell us why that stream ended. What it does show is more important: after eight encounters repeatedly treating the same left fifth-MTPJ endpoint, the Podiatry record ends while the foot problem itself continues through the neurological record for years afterward.

By then, the patient was again on procyclidine. Neurology already possessed the observation that withdrawing that drug had rapidly altered the outside-left-foot phenomenon. The mechanical stream had therefore accumulated repeated same-site evidence; the neurological stream had accumulated medication-state evidence; and the surviving record still contains no moment at which somebody takes professional ownership of the relationship between them.

10. 2019: the control specimen

Two years later another painful problem appears: severe shoulder and arm symptoms. Here the same neurologist behaves differently. The shoulder episode matters not because it is identical to the feet — it is not — but because it reveals professional capacity.

The differential opens. Nerve root. Brachial plexus. Shoulder pathology. Brachial neuritis. Muscle, tendon or ligament. Entrapment. Frozen shoulder. Even apical lung malignancy is considered as a remote serious possibility. Imaging is needed. The patient may not be able to keep still, so the practical barrier is not allowed to end the investigation; MRI under anaesthesia is arranged. Neurophysiology is considered. Negative imaging is understood to have limits.

Then come three sentences that are almost a miniature code of good diagnostic behaviour:

The actual cause for us is pretty important.
I genuinely do not know what the cause is here.
I will very often be wrong in terms of that assessment.

Most important of all for the later pregabalin story, Monaghan explicitly refuses to use limited gabapentin benefit as proof of cause. Shoulder, plexus, nerve root or something else might all improve because pain is mediated through nerves. In other words: a gabapentinoid response does not identify the disease.

This episode does not prove why the feet were treated differently. But it destroys one easy explanation. Monaghan was not incapable of open medicine. He knew how to hold uncertainty without turning it into closure. He knew how to say I do not know. He knew how to investigate across anatomical boundaries. He knew how to treat symptom response as non-specific evidence rather than diagnostic confirmation.

The shoulder episode therefore sits in this story like a control specimen placed beside an experiment. It shows what the clinician can do when the causal question remains genuinely open.

11. The feet keep crossing the boundary

By 2020 the feet are no longer behaving like a neatly peripheral afterthought even within the neurologist’s own notes. A bad corn remains on the outside of the left foot. The foot or lower limb again gives a foot-drop-like impression, but Monaghan writes that this is actually parkinsonian, supporting the conclusion with preserved dorsiflexion, plantarflexion, inversion and eversion. He documents exquisite sensitivity of the feet, excessive movement and an urge to move them.

Those are not Podiatry observations. They are neurological observations. The original partition is being crossed from inside Neurology itself.

In 2021 procyclidine is removed because of ocular concerns and Sinemet enters more substantially. The patient is very young and levodopa-sensitive. Chorea and dyskinesia are genuine concerns. Caution is therefore not, by itself, evidence of wrongdoing. But treatment caution is a different question from explanatory closure. A clinician can be entirely right to avoid aggressive levodopa escalation and still be required to reconsider what the feet mean.

By 2022 the feet are still sore in the morning, taking twenty to twenty-five minutes to warm up. Corn and callus pain remain. The patient wonders whether the pain may be neuralgic. The body is still supplying information to both sides of the partition: structure and neurological state.

A system genuinely learning from the patient would now have several opportunities to return to the founding question. Was the mechanical lesion being repeatedly loaded by abnormal motor control? Was dystonia involved? Was pain amplified by OFF state? Did the neurological treatment change the mechanical expression? Did the original completely separate possibility still deserve the same weight?

The record does not show that global reconstruction.

12. A strange sentence about DBS

In the 2021 record there is a small paragraph that becomes larger the longer one looks at it:

As I have reminded him, the DBS is only ever for the tremor element. It will not help with anything else.

There are three separate problems inside that sentence. First, the medical proposition is plainly unreliable: deep brain stimulation in Parkinson’s disease is not confined to tremor. It has long been used in carefully selected patients for levodopa-responsive motor complications, including dyskinesia and motor fluctuations, as well as tremor.

Second, this patient had repeatedly been characterised by the same clinician as lacking prominent tremor. The original phenotype was severe akinetic-rigid Parkinsonism with striking gait abnormality and very little tremor. Yet the paragraph continues by saying the patient would likely always remain a candidate for DBS. If DBS were truly only for tremor, the sentence conflicts with the clinician’s own description of the patient.

Third is the phrase As I have reminded him. That is not simply medical advice. It is an asserted memory of prior counselling. The longitudinal record presently supplies no earlier documented DBS reminder. That does not prove no conversation occurred. But it means the later reader is being asked to trust the author’s memory of what this particular patient had previously been told.

This belongs beside in his own mind – markedly divorced. In both places the record does more than report a clinical fact. It reports the history of understanding between clinician and patient. And once the reliability of that history matters to a later defence, these phrases become testable claims rather than neutral scenery.

The question is not theatrically was he thinking of another patient? The probative question is narrower: when a later note confidently remembers prior counselling that is medically dubious and internally awkward for this patient’s phenotype, how much evidential weight should later governance place on similarly retrospective descriptions of what had long been understood?

13. 2023: pain becomes a threat to survival

The history of the feet must not be flattened into one constant level of suffering. The early pain was severe, burning and deeply uncomfortable, but the patient lived with it. He worked. He adapted. He found ways around it. The later crisis was different.

By early 2023 the pain had changed scale and moral character.

On 1 February the patient writes directly to the neurologist, Parkinson’s nurse and GP. Lyrica has dulled the worst of the pain, but the residual state is absolutely intolerable though not unbearable. He says: I have no idea how I tolerated the pain, because I don’t think I could live with it anymore. Then he gives it the simplest possible name:

It is perfect torture.

He asks for the Lyrica to be increased.

Seven weeks later the temporary benefit has failed. On 22 March he writes to the GP practice:

When I say me feet are killing me I mean that literally. I’m losing the will to live with them at this stage.

And then:

the dosage worked for a day or two but now it isn’t enough.

The GP responds not with indifference but alarm. The urgent referral records severe bilateral burning pain in the feet and lower legs, extreme at time and causing death wish. Pregabalin, begun at 25 mg twice daily, has climbed through various doctors to 200 mg three times daily — 600 mg a day — and is described as only just controlling symptoms. The GP writes:

I am worried about him.

And:

I’d be grateful for your review before we get into trouble here.

Two days later the Movement Disorders nurse writes directly to Monaghan. The provenance could not be clearer:

He was commenced on Lyrica by his G.p and is currently taking 600mgs of Lyrica a day.

Then the key sentence:

Despite the Lyrica he states he is having debilitating pain and has no quality of life.

This is not a subtle signal. By this point the specialist service knows all of the following at once: the pain is centred in the same feet that began the story; it has escalated to death-wish language; the patient is at the maximum usual pregabalin dose; the drug’s benefit has been incomplete and unstable; and the GP is explicitly asking Neurology for help before the situation deteriorates further.

Then Neurology sees the patient and produces one of the most information-rich foot consultations in the record. Recurrent left lower-limb cramping raises the question of Parkinsonian dystonia whether an ON or OFF dystonia (clearly more likely an OFF dystonia). The foot pain is described as the major problem. There is major discomfort on the outside of the left foot. The patient cannot manage his fourth walk in the evening. A video of darts shows that he functions better when additional levodopa is on board. Monaghan says the ordinary bad evenings sound like typical OFF Parkinson’s evening symptoms.

The patient says he takes Stalevo for Pep in the Step and for pain. Monaghan explains how OFF state can magnify pain as a non-motor fluctuation and says this probably explains why the patient feels pain improves on Stalevo.

So by 2023 the old clean separation is being crossed by the treating neurologist’s own language: OFF symptoms, dystonia, levodopa-state effects, outside-left-foot discomfort.

Yet the old peripheral stream remains alive too. Fabry disease is investigated. Lyrica remains at 600 mg. The patient and consultant are described as having a misalignment. Monaghan characterises his own position as a firm belief that everything has to be explainable by Parkinson’s while acknowledging the patient’s very reasonable and justifiable concerns about the feet as a separate problem.

Then he writes:

It is not in the nature of a “peripheral neuropathy” to come on literally overnight, including notwithstanding that he had had foot pains/issues many years before going back to the beginning.

That sentence is medically interesting. It is also historically explosive, because Monaghan already possessed the 2018 observation that the outside-left-foot phenomenon changed changing and changing rapidly when procyclidine was withdrawn.

By 2023, therefore, rapid state-dependent change cannot be treated as an alien possibility. It is already part of the patient's recorded biology.

And in the same consultation appears the sentence that changes the professional atmosphere:

While it would be embarrassing if there was a cause other than iPD, we have a much much larger interest in his diagnosis being right as opposed to me being right.

No backward motive need be invented. The clinician has named the professional dimension himself. The diagnostic history now has personal and institutional weight.

14. The psychological record is part of the medical record

The two years between the 2023 crisis and the controlled-release breakthrough must not fade into the background as if psychological suffering were merely an emotional annotation to a physical symptom.

The distress is documented because the pain is changing what the patient can bear.

In 2023 he says he is losing the will to live. The GP records death wish. The nurse records no quality of life. Pregabalin reaches 600 mg a day. The patient has described becoming afraid to come off it — not because he regarded the drug as a cure, but because he was afraid for his own safety if the feet became worse again. The fear is not abstract dependence language. It is the fear of losing the only partial barrier then standing between him and a pain he had already described as perfect torture.

In 2024 the record sounds calmer, but the life described inside it is shrinking. The patient says life is a lot smaller. Folding clothes takes too long, especially in the evening. Seven minutes pumping bicycle tyres brings the toes curling in. Darts collapse from hours to roughly ninety minutes a week because standing worsens the foot pain. Lyrica has improved the right foot and some of the left-foot pain, but it has not resolved the toe curling and has not restored the old life.

Temporary improvement matters. It does not erase the crisis that preceded it, and it does not turn the drug into a causal diagnosis.

By March 2025 the patient writes a briefing for his GP because he is afraid a telephone conversation will not adequately convey what is happening. The language is not clinical ornament. It is an inventory of cumulative injury:

Turned mornings into psychological ambushes.
Drained my cognitive resilience.
Subtly conditioned me to fear movement.
Worn down my psychological endurance.

Then:

the fear of falling and the realization that, if I did, I may not want to get back up

And finally:

I am running out of psychological endurance for this.

This is the intervening reality between Not clear and the later therapeutic breakthrough. The unresolved relationship is not sitting harmlessly in a file. It has become the framework within which treatment options are interpreted while a patient tries to survive the symptom.

The clinical note from the same period gives the body’s version. The one thing on the patient's mind is foot pain. Since 2005, my feet disturb me. Each morning is murder in the feet. The first 150 footsteps are torture. The phrase I won’t live with it is used to put words on the situation.

Then comes the fact that should make the reader stop:

Once the Parkinsons medications hit his system he does have a relief in the feelings in the feet.

The neurologist knows this. He goes further:

He knows very well that once the Sinemet hits his system that the feet feel better from a pain perspective.

Yet the governing explanatory rule remains:

pain is not really a direct part of Parkinson’s at all.

Dystonia is acknowledged as potentially painful. Further Sinemet is expected to provide further relief. But the feared dyskinesias constrain immediate-release escalation, and the proposed alternative is again to consider increasing Lyrica — the same drug which at 600 mg had never eliminated the pain.

This is why the later controlled-release result is not simply an interesting response to medication. It arrives after two years in which the symptom has become a psychological threat, the neuropathic-pain drug has reached its ceiling, and the patient’s life has reorganised itself around avoiding a pain whose relationship to the movement disorder remained professionally unresolved.

15. Put 2019 and 2023 side by side

This is where the shoulder control specimen becomes most illuminating.

In 2019, confronted with pain of uncertain cause, Monaghan says:

I genuinely do not know what the cause is here.

Uncertainty produces a differential. It produces imaging. It produces workarounds. It produces humility.

In 2023, confronted with the feet, he now knows vastly more than he did at discharge. He knows the outside-left-foot phenomenon changed rapidly during procyclidine withdrawal. He has later called the foot throw actually parkinsonian. He has documented exquisite foot sensitivity and excessive movement. The Parkinson’s service has recognised OFF-related pain and shuffling. He now explicitly entertains Parkinsonian dystonia and considers OFF dystonia more likely than ON dystonia. The patient reports taking Stalevo for pain.

Then Monaghan writes:

It is not in the nature of a “peripheral neuropathy” to come on literally overnight.

That sentence is fascinating because it is medically capable of being true while still creating a problem for the old partition. A fixed neuropathy does not ordinarily materialise overnight. But in 2018 Monaghan had already recorded a rapid neurological-drug-linked change in the very outside-left-foot phenomenon. By 2023, therefore, he possesses evidence that at least part of what the patient experiences at that site is state-dependent.

The question becomes unavoidable: if rapid change argues against a newly appearing peripheral neuropathy, what explanatory significance did the earlier rapid procyclidine-linked return have?

Instead of visibly returning to the original PRKN/dystonia-foot relationship, the record explores Fabry disease — a rare condition capable of accommodating both Parkinsonism and neuropathic symptoms.

And there, in the same consultation, appears another remarkable sentence:

While it would be embarrassing if there was a cause other than iPD, we have a much much larger interest in his diagnosis being right as opposed to me being right.

The second half is an ethical declaration. The first half is still evidence. We need not invent professional embarrassment; the clinician names it himself.

16. The word “embarrassing” changes the atmosphere

No responsible reading should project the 2023 sentence backward and announce that embarrassment caused the 2017 decision. That would outrun the evidence. But it would be equally irresponsible to pretend the word has no significance.

By 2023 the clinician himself frames diagnostic error partly in terms of professional embarrassment: it would be embarrassing if there was a cause other than iPD. He then immediately insists, correctly, that the diagnosis being right matters more than him being right. The very need to make that distinction reveals that the two things — correctness of diagnosis and correctness of clinician — are psychologically and professionally separable in his own account.

Now return to 2019. There, the clinician can say I will very often be wrong with almost effortless freedom. The shoulder diagnosis carries no eight-year institutional history. No founding discharge architecture depends on it. No complaint has yet asked whether the entire pathway rested on a false separation. Being wrong about the shoulder is simply medicine.

By 2023, being wrong about the disease formulation has history attached to it. Years of letters. Years of treatment decisions. Years of terminology. Years in which PRKN has faded and iPD has hardened. The patient and neurologist now explicitly have a misalignment: Monaghan describes his own position as a firm belief that everything must be explainable by Parkinson’s, while the patient has reasonable and justifiable concerns about the feet as a separate problem.

The irony is almost perfect. The 2017 document had helped place the feet outside the neurological syndrome. By 2023 Monaghan describes himself as believing everything must be explainable by Parkinson’s — yet the peripheral neuropathy/Fabry route remains available when the feet do not fit comfortably.

The problem is no longer simply diagnosis. It is the stability of a professional narrative under pressure from accumulating biology.

17. PRKN enters clearly — and then disappears without a visible exit

The genetic chronology is unusually clean at the beginning. Very young onset leads to genetic concern. Monaghan seeks Healy’s advice. Healy says the patient probably has Park II. PARK2 testing is recommended. In 2018 Monaghan writes explicitly of the PARK 2 PRKN which we/Prof Healy suspect is the cause. He discusses why a genetic diagnosis might matter: neuropathology, prognosis, levodopa sensitivity.

Then the trail becomes strange. The hypothesis does not appear to be formally disproved in the record we have. No negative PRKN result has been found. No explicit clinic letter says: we previously suspected PARK2, but evidence X, Y and Z now make idiopathic Parkinson’s disease more likely. No documented bridge has been identified.

Nevertheless, by 2023 and especially by the 2025 rebuttal, idiopathic Parkinson’s disease is no longer tentative shorthand. It is presented as the department’s belief and understanding.

This is not a small terminological curiosity. Idiopathic means no identified specific cause. PRKN is a specific genetic cause. A record can use labels loosely in ordinary clinical life, but once governance later relies on historical documentation as evidence of what was professionally understood at each moment, the missing transition matters.

When did PRKN stop being the working explanation? What evidence displaced it? Who made that decision? Was testing deferred, declined, lost, never sent, or negative? If none of those things occurred, did a convenient clinical label simply harden into an etiological conclusion?

In February 2026 the answer arrives from outside the old architecture: two pathogenic Parkin variants, compound heterozygous, with Parkinsonism and dystonia attributed to PRKN.

The 2026 result does not magically prove every earlier criticism. It does something more exact. It confirms that the specific territory identified in 2017–2018 was real, while exposing how poorly the record explains its own later departure from that territory.

18. Pregabalin: how a messy history becomes a clean strategy

The 2025 complaint rebuttal contains this description:

His Lyrica pregabalin treatment was introduced for the neuropathic pain, of whatever cause, in part to try to spare further Sinemet increase.

Read alone, that sentence is elegant. It presents a coherent therapeutic strategy: neuropathic pain appears; pregabalin is introduced; the drug also helps avoid excessive levodopa in a young, dyskinesia-prone patient.

But the contemporaneous chronology is less tidy. Primary care started Lyrica. The dose climbed through various doctors. The Parkinson’s nurse explicitly told Monaghan that the GP had commenced it. At 600 mg per day the patient could still be in pain severe enough to speak of a death wish. Later there were periods of partial benefit, and Neurology could quite reasonably have continued or preferred pregabalin partly to avoid escalating immediate-release levodopa. But that later rationale is not the same thing as the reason the drug was originally introduced.

This is a miniature example of retrospective coherence. A sequence of events with changing ownership and changing reasons becomes compressed into one clinically sensible sentence.

That compression may be innocent. It is still evidentially important because the later institutional defence depends heavily on documents as reliable accounts of what was understood and why decisions were made.

And the 2019 shoulder episode returns once more. Even if pregabalin truly helped, Monaghan himself had already explained that such a response does not identify cause. The same rule that protected the shoulder differential should have protected the feet from being over-assigned to neuropathy.

By 2025 the patient had even reduced Lyrica substantially, yet foot pain returned to the centre of the consultation. The explanation was still being negotiated long after the original Not clear had functioned as practical closure.

19. March 2025: the old architecture meets a new experiment

By March 2025 the record is almost impossibly rich. The feet, mainly the left, are again the central concern. Mornings are murder in the feet. The first hundred or more steps are torture. The patient describes localised sharp left-foot pain, pressure-sensitive and aggravated by movement. He wakes pain-free; standing activates the problem. Parkinson’s medication reaches the system and the feet improve.

Monaghan acknowledges several things at once. Sinemet should not, in his view, directly treat pain. Parkinson’s can alter the experience of pain. Dystonia can be painful. The described pain is nevertheless not, in his opinion, direct Parkinsonian phenomenology. Yet he has little doubt that more Sinemet would provide further relief, restrained by the risk of dyskinesia.

Then controlled-release Sinemet enters.

The patient reports that the controlled-release preparation changed the disabling foot pain essentially overnight, and his formal complaint records immediate and sustained relief from two decades of disabling foot pain. The later clinician account does not dispute the magnitude of the result; it calls the response profound relief currently. Importantly, the later treatment history is not simply more levodopa equals less pain. The patient reports that immediate-release Sinemet given repeatedly could aggravate the state, while controlled-release delivery changed the experience dramatically. He would soon become unable to tolerate even one full 12.5/50 immediate-release tablet. The variable appears to include the shape of dopaminergic delivery over time.

The neurologist later calls the CR result:

profound relief currently.

This is the point at which the old architecture should be placed under maximum stress. A static peripheral explanation must now coexist with years of medication-state changes, OFF-related pain, dystonia reasoning, procyclidine effects, Stalevo effects and a preparation-specific levodopa response.

And precisely at this moment, the clinician is also required to answer a complaint about the historical care.

20. When fresh clinical evidence arrives during a defence

This coincidence is professionally delicate. The problem is not that a doctor defending earlier care becomes incapable of thinking medically. The problem is that two different tasks now compete.

Task one is clinical: what does the new CR response mean? Does it alter the model? Does it increase the probability of dystonia, OFF-state pain, abnormal motor loading or some interaction between local structure and dopaminergic state? Does it require a new specialist review?

Task two is procedural: explain why the previous care was reasonable.

In the rebuttal, the new result is largely absorbed into continuity. The letters, Monaghan says, show that it had been understood for quite some time that Sinemet helped pain. The CR response becomes further evidence that the existing account had always accommodated the phenomenon.

That is a profound documentary move. A new observation that could destabilise the old formulation is instead used to demonstrate that the old formulation already knew about it.

The same happens with pregabalin. A messy primary-care initiation, dose escalation, maximum-dose failure and later Neurology adoption becomes a coherent levodopa-sparing strategy. The same happens with iPD: an unexplained drift from prior PRKN suspicion becomes the department’s established belief and understanding.

None of these sentences has to be consciously deceptive to have this effect. Institutions often produce coherence retrospectively because coherence is what a defence requires. But medicine is supposed to remain able to say: this new fact changes what I think the old facts meant.

THE CRUCIAL QUESTION IS WHETHER THE NEW EVIDENCE WAS ALLOWED TO CHANGE THE STORY — OR ONLY TO BE FITTED INTO IT.

21. The GP is trapped downstream too

The information architecture does not constrain only the patient.

It constrains primary care.

This matters because it answers a tempting but shallow question: if the patient was deteriorating so badly, why did the GP not simply change the Parkinson’s medication?

By August 2025 the answer is explicit in writing. The patient asks the GP for help while awaiting specialist review. The GP replies:

I am certainly minded to help but as a GP I cannot really be adjusting your levodopa meds without Neurology specialist guidance as that would be well beyond my ordinary scope of expertise.

That is not a failure of compassion. It is a statement of professional hierarchy.

The GP had been the clinician who originally saw the integrated problem. He had observed abnormal feet, gait, tone, toe clawing and deterioration and sent the patient upward precisely because the neurological relationship required specialist interpretation. After Neurology, however, the GP is downstream of the specialist information state.

Now the importance of consultant Not clear becomes concrete.

The specialist formulation does not merely influence what the patient believes. It helps define what a GP can safely and professionally do. The GP can prescribe within ordinary primary care. The GP can escalate pain treatment. The GP can refer urgently. The GP can say I am worried about him. But when the potential solution lies in adjusting a highly specialised dopaminergic regimen in a very-young-onset, dyskinesia-sensitive patient, the GP cannot simply overrule Neurology and experiment independently.

So the distortion becomes two-sided.

The patient is living inside a clinical reality in which the feet have been assigned uncertain/peripheral status despite repeated neurological crossings. The GP is living inside a professional reality in which the specialist owns the dopaminergic boundary.

Both can see suffering.

Neither can independently reconstruct the specialist model that governs the treatment.

THE INFORMATION STATE CAN PARALYSE PRIMARY CARE ALMOST AS EFFECTIVELY AS THE PAIN PARALYSES THE PATIENT.

That is why the authority of the founding record is not abstract. It determines what later clinicians believe they are authorised to question, what they are authorised to change, and when they must send the patient back into the same specialist architecture for permission to cross its boundaries.

22. Backstop Two: the record becomes evidence for itself

The March 2025 rebuttal is the second great documentary event in the case.

The first backstop was the 2017 discharge synthesis. It established the architecture under which the feet and Parkinsonism could continue on partly separate tracks while the relationship remained Not clear.

The years between are the evidential corridor. New observations repeatedly cross the boundary. Some are accommodated. Some are explained as OFF state. Some are called dystonia. Some are treated mechanically. Some respond to neurological medication. None visibly compels a wholesale reconstruction of the founding relation.

Then the patient challenges the architecture itself.

The consultant’s rebuttal looks backward through the corridor and finds continuity. The letters, he says, show a longstanding understanding that Sinemet helps the pain. The caution around levodopa is presented as deliberate, sustained and medically principled. Pregabalin is described as treatment for neuropathic pain of whatever cause, introduced in part to spare further Sinemet increase. The current CR response is accepted but placed inside the old explanatory model: levodopa can improve the experience of pain without meaning the pain itself is directly Parkinsonian.

Each individual proposition can sound coherent.

The structural question is whether the coherence is independent evidence or merely persistence.

If later observations were repeatedly interpreted through the founding architecture, their compatibility with that architecture cannot automatically prove that the founding architecture was independently correct.

The loop looks like this:

an architecture forms; the patient challenges closure; the challenge does not enter the durable synthesis; the architecture becomes the operative plan; later observations are interpreted inside it; those interpretations accumulate into continuity; years later the continuity is cited as evidence that the architecture was coherent all along.

At that point the record begins to validate itself.

This is especially visible in the controlled-release result. A new response capable of destabilising the historic separation is narrated as something already understood: As the letters show, we have had a clear understanding for quite some time that Sinemet does help his pain.

That sentence converts surprise into continuity.

The same occurs with pregabalin. The contemporaneous record says the GP commenced it and the dose climbed through various doctors. The later rebuttal incorporates the drug into a cleaner Neurology strategy. The later account may describe why Neurology continued to value pregabalin; it cannot retroactively change who introduced it or why it entered the case in the first place.

The problem is not that retrospective synthesis is forbidden. Medicine constantly synthesises history. The problem is that retrospective coherence becomes dangerous when the very reliability of the historical synthesis is the matter under dispute.

WHEN CONTINUITY IS PRODUCED BY REPEATEDLY INTERPRETING NEW FACTS THROUGH THE OLD MODEL, CONTINUITY MAY PROVE PERSISTENCE BEFORE IT PROVES CORRECTNESS.

23. A medical record has two lives

By the time formal governance becomes involved, the record has acquired a second life.

Its first life is historical. It records what clinicians wrote, believed and did at particular moments.

Its second life is operational. It tells future clinicians what to assume, tells primary care where specialist boundaries lie, provides the background for treatment decisions, frames complaints, supplies evidence to incident review, and determines what later facts must overcome in order to change the inherited story.

The hospital’s later Article 16 reasoning makes this explicit. A diagnosis in a medical file, it says, reflects the professional opinion of the doctor at that moment of time. A prior diagnosis provides important context for later treatment decisions. Maintaining the record serves the public interest by providing:

a reliable record of what has taken place.

This institutional premise makes the documentary inquiry unavoidable.

If the record is merely an old historical artefact, its imperfections may be unfortunate but limited. If the record is infrastructure for continuity of care, then the way it arranges uncertainty matters because that arrangement continues to act on the patient.

The hospital cannot simultaneously say the historical document remains important because it explains later treatment and then treat questions about the document’s omissions, retrospective synthesis and inherited assumptions as though they concern a dead past.

The record is not only memory. It is infrastructure.

And the infrastructure built here contains several points now capable of being tested: the absence of the Protest; the post-discharge finalisation; the clinician-amplified markedly divorced; the missing exit condition attached to Not clear; the unbridged PRKN-to-iPD transition; the later retrospective pregabalin narrative; the asserted DBS memory; the repeated incorporation of neurological foot responses without global reconstruction.

Governance then reaches its conclusion:

there was no deficit in the care provided.

The reasoning gives considerable emphasis to the caution used around Sinemet in a very young patient at risk of dyskinesia.

That may answer an important question. It does not necessarily answer the central one.

Was cautious levodopa prescribing defensible?

And:

Was the feet–dystonia–PRKN relationship adequately integrated, reopened and transferred when necessary?

Those are not the same question.

A review can correctly conclude that aggressive levodopa would have carried risks and still fail to answer why an unresolved movement-disorders relationship became operationally settled.

24. The original fork returns at institutional scale

In January 2026 the institution states that the subsequent dystonia diagnosis does not undermine the validity of the earlier processes or outcomes. The updated diagnosis does not, it says, necessitate reopening governance reviews, altering prior determinations or retrospectively amending historical medical records. No current governance risk is identified from continued reliance on the completed processes.

The sentence is remarkable because it recreates the original fork at a larger scale.

In 2017 the question was: will a newly exposed medical incompleteness reopen the pathway, or will the pathway survive while the uncertainty is absorbed into the record?

In 2026 the question becomes: will later specialist confirmation reopen the historical governance model, or will governance survive while the new diagnosis is treated as a subsequent clinical update?

The answer, institutionally, is again closure.

There is an obvious defence: hindsight is dangerous. A later diagnosis does not automatically make every earlier decision negligent. Historical clinicians must be judged on information available to them at the time.

But this case has a special feature. Dystonia and PRKN are not alien concepts introduced from the future. They are already present in the original department’s own 2017–2018 reasoning. The finalised 2017 document describes a gait suggestive of dystonic posture. The treating neurologist identifies PRKN/PARK2 as a serious possibility. Healy says the patient probably has Park II. In 2018 Monaghan writes of the specific PARK 2 PRKN which we/Prof Healy suspect is the cause.

So when outside specialists later confirm dystonia and biallelic PRKN, the question is not whether governance should punish 2017 clinicians for failing to predict an unimaginable future.

The question is whether the later confirmation should force a new look at why concepts already inside the original information state failed to reorganise the patient’s pathway when the patient himself had protested that the feet remained unfinished.

The biology updated.

The documentary architecture did not visibly reconstruct itself around the update.

That is why the case ends where it began: at the ethics of reopening.

25. The ethical question underneath everything

Strip away the genes, the medications, the complaints machinery and the specialist vocabulary. The ethical question is almost childishly simple.

A patient enters hospital with painful feet, abnormal gait and pathological movement. Neurology discovers severe young-onset Parkinsonism. Near discharge the patient says: you have not properly investigated the feet; you are not finished. What is the proper professional response?

The answer cannot be merely that Podiatry is a legitimate specialty. Of course it is. The answer cannot be merely that levodopa has risks. Of course it does. The answer cannot be that the feet might have a mechanical lesion. They did — and Podiatry proved that with unusual consistency, finding the same left fifth-MTPJ endpoint at every one of eight documented encounters. But repeated local proof of a mechanical consequence made the unanswered neurological relationship more important, not less. The answer must address the relationship the patient was asking about.

Ethical medicine, at that fork, means permitting the patient’s objection to alter the clinical state of play. It means that closure has to be earned medically, not merely made possible procedurally. It means an admitted limit of expertise causes something. It means uncertainty belongs to an action plan, not just a paragraph. It means a later observation that threatens the model is allowed to threaten it.

The professional danger is subtler. A consultant can be intelligent, technically capable and even conscientious, yet still become captured by the architecture of an earlier decision. Once a record has been written, a clinic organised around it, a treatment strategy justified by it and later correspondence layered on top, the cost of reopening the founding premise rises. Not necessarily because anyone is corrupt. Because systems defend coherence.

The Protest is therefore the moral centre of the case. It is the point at which the patient says, in effect: the story you are about to close does not yet explain my body.

Everything that follows asks whether the system listened medically or merely heard procedurally.

26. What the later biology says about the fork

The years after 2017 behave like a long experiment conducted by the patient’s own nervous system.

Stop procyclidine and the body changes; the walk begins to fall apart; the outside-left-foot phenomenon returns rapidly. Across eight documented Podiatry encounters from September 2017 to March 2019, the same left fifth-MTPJ mechanical endpoint is found eight times and repeatedly debrided, enucleated or offloaded. Then the Podiatry documentary stream ends while the neurological story continues. Neurology later calls the foot throw Parkinsonian. The feet become exquisitely sensitive and restless. OFF states alter walking and pain. Stalevo helps enough that the patient takes it for pain. The neurologist considers OFF dystonia. Pregabalin fails to provide stable control even at maximum dose. Controlled-release levodopa later produces profound relief. Finally, an outside specialist diagnoses dystonia and genetics confirms biallelic PRKN.

No one item is required to carry the whole conclusion. Their power lies in accumulation.

The founding partition said, in effect, that the painful feet might be separate, perhaps peripheral, and were suitable for Podiatry while Neurology pursued Parkinsonism. The later biology repeatedly refuses to respect that clean boundary.

And this is where the phrase markedly divorced performs its second function. In 2017 it helps stabilise separation. Read years later, after neurological medication repeatedly alters the feet, it exposes how much causal force the document had placed on a patient’s sensory distinction.

The same is true of Not clear. In 2017 it looks cautious. Years later it looks like an unresolved question that was permitted to travel forward without an owner.

The same is true of I am not a movement disorders neurologist. At first it reads as humility. Years later, when the movement-disorders problem remains unresolved, it becomes a question about what humility was operationally worth.

The quotations do not change. Their evidential function does.

27. The questions the record should now be made to answer

  1. What exactly happened clinically after the Protest? Was the patient re-examined neurologically, were the feet remapped, was discharge reconsidered, and where is that work recorded?
  2. When did the dystonic-posture / NOT FOOT DROP formulation crystallise? Before the Protest, after it, or during post-discharge finalisation?
  3. Why did the final durable document preserve “in his own mind – markedly divorced” but not preserve the patient's objection that the feet remained neurologically unfinished?
  4. What operational consequence followed “I am not a movement disorders neurologist”? Not genetics advice: movement-disorders referral, shared care, transfer or specialist reassessment.
  5. How was the April 2018 “changing and changing rapidly” outside-left-foot phenomenon interpreted at the time? Was Podiatry ever told that neurological drug withdrawal altered the same territory it found at all eight documented treatment encounters? Conversely, were Neurology ever asked to explain why Podiatry kept removing and offloading essentially the same left fifth-MTPJ lesion — including two enucleations producing bleeding — while the patient continued to experience the same territory as a problem?
  6. Why did the 2019 shoulder uncertainty generate such disciplined causal investigation while later foot uncertainty repeatedly failed to trigger a global reconstruction?
  7. When Monaghan wrote in 2023 that peripheral neuropathy does not come on literally overnight, what explanatory significance did he give his own 2018 observation of a rapid medication-linked outside-left-foot change?
  8. What happened to PRKN? What evidence transformed “probably PARK II” and “PARK2 PRKN which we/Prof Healy suspect is the cause” into a settled departmental belief in iPD?
  9. What is the provenance of the 2021 DBS counselling statement? What prior conversation was being “reminded,” and how does “DBS is only ever for tremor” coexist with this patient's lack of prominent tremor and claimed continuing candidacy?
  10. Who introduced pregabalin and why? How did a GP-initiated, incompletely effective treatment later become narrated as having been introduced partly to spare Sinemet?
  11. What diagnostic reconsideration followed the acknowledged “profound relief” from Sinemet CR? Was it treated as fresh evidence or as proof that the old narrative had always been sufficient?
  12. Did Clinical Governance actually examine the feet–dystonia–PRKN integration question? Or did it establish that cautious levodopa prescribing was reasonable and allow that answer to stand in for the broader issue?

28. What ethical medicine would have protected

The strongest version of this case does not depend on pretending every decision was foolish. Quite the opposite.

Podiatry was capable of treating real mechanical pathology. Levodopa carried real dyskinesia risk. The patient was genuinely treatment-sensitive. Pregabalin could produce periods of partial benefit. Pain in Parkinsonism can be complicated. The later PRKN result does not mean every corn was genetically caused or every sensation was dystonia.

Those facts do not weaken the central proposition. They show why the ethical duty was to preserve the relationship as a live question rather than permit uncertainty to become a professional destination.

Ethical medicine would have protected several things.

It would have protected the patient’s Protest as clinically material information rather than allowing the durable account to proceed without it.

It would have protected uncertainty by giving it an exit condition: what future observation would make us reopen this?

It would have protected the difference between symptom phenomenology and causal inference: feeling foot pain differently from leg tension does not prove two unrelated diseases.

It would have protected the consequences of admitted expertise limits. I am not a movement disorders neurologist should be capable of causing a movement-disorders action.

It would have protected causal curiosity when neurological medication changed the exact foot territory under mechanical treatment.

It would have protected the patient from being asked to live indefinitely inside a partition merely because each new contradiction could be explained locally.

And once the pain became psychologically dangerous, ethics would have required the severity itself to alter the urgency with which the unresolved relationship was revisited.

The record documents that progression. Perfect torture. losing the will to live. death wish. no quality of life. life is a lot smaller. murder in the feet. The first 150 footsteps are torture. I won’t live with it.

Those are not literary effects added after the fact. They are the patient, GP, nurse and neurologist describing the clinical consequences while care is ongoing.

Then controlled-release Sinemet produces what the neurologist himself calls:

profound relief currently.

The ethical question at that moment is not whether one response proves every mechanism. The ethical question is what a clinician and an institution owe a patient when a treatment result radically changes the lived reality of a symptom that had become dangerous, and when that result sits inside a long chain of earlier neurological crossings.

Does the new fact reopen the old question?

Or does the old record explain the new fact away quickly enough to preserve itself?

The story does not end because genetics wins an argument. It ends because after all of this, one human being has to decide what to do with a history that the institutions around him have repeatedly declined to reconstruct in the way he believes the evidence demands.

30. The vow

There came a day, after the Sinemet CR had been taken and the relief had arrived, when I understood something almost as clearly as I understood the absence of the pain itself.

Nobody was interested in my feet.

Not interested in what it meant that pain which had driven me towards the edge of my own psychological endurance could change so profoundly. Not interested in going back to the beginning. Not interested in asking what the feet had been trying to tell us in 2005, in 2017, during the Procyclidine withdrawal, through years of Podiatry, through OFF periods, through Lyrica, through the first tortured footsteps of every morning.

The pain had changed.

The history had not.

And on that day I made myself a promise.

I would get to the root of it.

I would go back through the referrals, the letters, the medications, the phrases, the omissions, the things people remembered and the things the record said had happened. I would follow the feet through every document. I would find the point at which the medicine divided from the patient, and I would find out what happened after that.

I would get to the centre.

Because by then I understood that there was one witness who had been present for the entire thirteen-year prodrome, the eight-day admission, the Protest, the treatment responses, the years of pain, the psychological deterioration and the eventual relief.

The patient.

Me.

The hospital had its record.

The doctors had their letters.

Governance had its conclusions.

But I had lived the experiment.

I decided that I would understand it well enough to tell the story in a way nobody else could tell it, because nobody else had occupied the one place from which every part of it could be seen at once.

This is that story.
