# 2023 Convergence Crisis and Safety Integration

**Status:** SOURCE-LEVEL ANALYTICAL LAYER — 8 September 2026  
**Purpose:** Distinguish the 2023 episode from the older 2017 foot-genealogy question and ask whether pain, mobility, falls risk, medication burden and psychological endurance were recognised and managed as one contemporaneous safety object.

---

## 1. Why 2023 requires its own analysis

The 2023 episode should not be treated merely as another later fact that crossed the 2017 feet–Neurology boundary.

By 2023 several clinically important streams converge at once:

- severe bilateral foot/lower-leg pain;
- major deterioration in walking and activity;
- high-dose pregabalin exposure;
- OFF-state / dystonic possibilities;
- longstanding Parkinsonian gait disorder;
- directly observed stumbles;
- alcohol/cannabis factors capable of complicating interpretation and balance;
- and unusually serious psychological-endurance language in the urgent referral pathway.

The resulting question is broader than:

> **Was the foot mechanism reconsidered?**

It is:

> **Was the combined pain–mobility–falls–psychological safety state recognised and managed as one clinical object?**

This is a separate inquiry from whether the 2017 founding foot allocation was negligent.

---

## 2. Falls risk was already present in the longitudinal neurological information state

The 2017 neurological history records:

> **“Balance is very bad — hasn't fallen yet”**

and the patient's belief that:

> **“a fall is imminent.”**

See `CLIN-000952–000969`.

Later, in the 2019 Neurology record, the GP's concern that the patient **“might fall”** is expressly described as understandable because Parkinsonian gait-disorder elements were visible. The neurologist writes:

> **“Certainly [Patient] might fall”**

and treats worsening balance / imbalance as relevant to gabapentin escalation. The same passage also recognises the interaction of gabapentinoids and alcohol and notes falls/balance concerns in that context. See `CLIN-002042–002068`.

Thus the 2023 stumbles did not arise in a documentary vacuum.

The longitudinal information state already contained:

> **poor balance → anticipated fall → recognised Parkinsonian gait disorder → acknowledged possibility of falling.**

---

## 3. March 2023 begins as an urgent pain and psychological-endurance crisis

The **26 March 2023** GP referral is marked **Urgent** and addressed to the treating neurologist. It reports severe bilateral burning pain in the feet and lower legs, says the pain was at times extreme and **“causing death wish,”** records escalating pregabalin exposure, states **“I am worried about him,”** and asks for review **“before we get into trouble here.”** See `CLIN-002623–002670`.

On **28 March 2023**, the Parkinson's nurse writes directly to the consultant that the patient is taking **600 mg/day Lyrica**, yet reports **debilitating pain**, **“no quality of life,”** and worsening over months. See `CLIN-002675–002688`.

These statements do not prove suicidal intent, a plan or a psychiatric diagnosis.

Their safe significance is:

> **By late March 2023 the treating neurological channel had been alerted to a severe pain crisis with explicit death/endurance language and major quality-of-life deterioration.**

---

## 4. The later 2023 clinic review shows that the crisis had changed, not vanished

The later Neurology consultation should be described accurately.

The note records that the patient was seen in clinic and that the consultation lasted about **an hour and 20 minutes**. At that visit the clinician records a positive mood and says the patient looked medically well. Therefore do **not** convert the March urgent-referral language into a claim of an unchanged continuous suicidal state at the consultation. See `CLIN-002420–002445`.

But the same consultation records a major preceding functional crisis:

- the patient had **“ground to an absolute halt”** with bilateral foot pain;
- there had been **three bad bouts where he gave up**;
- walking had been scaled back;
- a fourth evening walk was not possible;
- clawing formed part of the walking problem;
- lower-limb cramping raised Parkinsonian dystonia, with OFF dystonia considered more likely;
- evenings could involve protective stance, lack of movement and short steps.

See `CLIN-002424–002457`.

Thus:

> **The acute psychological language had improved by the clinic visit, but the physical and functional crisis remained a central part of the clinical history being reviewed.**

---

## 5. The gait examination was not negative

The 2023 note states:

> **“Superficially, the gait actually looks okay though I did see some stumbles.”**

It further records mild choreiform features with longer observation. A video reviewed during the consultation also contained movements described as stumbles / extra steps. See `CLIN-002445–002457`.

This does not permit a numerical estimate of the patient's probability of falling.

It does establish:

> **Directly observed instability during a brief neurological gait assessment.**

That finding has to be interpreted against the pre-existing information state of poor balance, anticipated falling, known Parkinsonian gait disorder and earlier clinician acknowledgement that the patient might fall.

---

## 6. What the corridor-style gait assessment could and could not test

A short neurological gait assessment can inform questions such as:

- bradykinesia;
- freezing / hesitation;
- stride and arm swing;
- dyskinesia;
- gross dystonic or OFF-state motor behaviour;
- observable instability.

It is not, by itself, a focused examination of:

- recurrent fifth-MTPJ loading;
- plantar/lateral pressure distribution;
- activity-dependent toe curling over sustained exertion;
- the shoe–foot interface;
- the relationship between dystonic/postural state and focal forefoot loading;
- or the exact Podiatry mechanical object developed during 2017–2019.

The important point is therefore two-sided:

> **The gait examination was too nonspecific to demonstrate that the focal fifth-MTPJ problem had been investigated, but it was specific enough to reveal a separate positive safety finding: stumbles.**

---

## 7. The 2023 consultation decomposes the crisis into branches

The consultation contains extensive reasoning about:

- OFF-state walking;
- levodopa / Stalevo effect;
- pain modulation;
- possible Parkinsonian dystonia;
- peripheral neuropathy;
- Fabry disease;
- pregabalin;
- alcohol;
- cannabis;
- repeat DATscan.

See `CLIN-002458–002495`.

Each branch is clinically intelligible.

The broader audit question is whether the crisis was also held together as one risk state:

> **pain + impaired walking + observed instability + medication burden + psychological endurance.**

This is the same information-arrangement problem seen elsewhere in the case, but it is a new contemporaneous safety question in its own right.

---

## 8. What response is visible to the observed instability

The 2023 recommendations visibly include:

- reduce alcohol substantially;
- reduce smoking;
- caution about cannabis;
- continue Lyrica 600 mg for the time being;
- possible additional neuropathic-pain medication;
- possible adjustment of evening levodopa;
- Fabry-related testing;
- repeat DATscan reasoning.

See `CLIN-002483–002495`.

The patient-facing consultation presently reviewed does **not identify** a specific response framed as:

- formal falls-risk assessment;
- targeted balance assessment;
- Parkinson's-specific physiotherapy referral for the observed instability;
- gait/balance rehabilitation plan;
- walking-aid / assistive-device assessment;
- home/environmental falls review;
- explicit falls-safety counselling;
- or a defined monitoring/escalation criterion tied to further stumbling or falling.

This is a record-audit finding, not yet a standard-of-care conclusion.

The correct expert question is:

> **In a patient with established Parkinsonian gait disorder, prior balance concerns, severe current mobility restriction, high-dose pregabalin exposure and directly observed stumbles during a recent urgent pain crisis, what assessment and falls-prevention response would ordinarily be expected?**

---

## 9. The 2023 crisis should not be reduced to the foot-genealogy argument

The 2023 consultation is relevant to the 2017 case because it contains later boundary-crossing evidence.

But it should also be audited independently.

The source supports a second integration question:

> **Did the treating service integrate the combined physical, functional and psychological safety consequences of the current presentation, irrespective of what had happened in 2017?**

That prevents the later crisis from being used merely as retrospective proof about the founding admission.

---

## 10. March 2025 shows the mobility–pain–psychological thread becoming explicit again

The **10 March 2025 Neurology clinic letter** records severe morning foot pain, the first 150 steps as **“torture,”** and states:

> **“To say ‘I won’t live with it’ allows him put words on the situation.”**

The same passage records that once Parkinson's medication reaches the system there is relief in the feet. See `CLIN-002776–002780`; date anchor `CLIN-005923–005925`.

This language should not be converted automatically into suicidal intent. It is recorded as the patient's way of putting the intolerability of the situation into words.

Eight days later, in the **18 March 2025 GP briefing**, the patient writes of:

> **“The fear of falling and the realization that if I did, I may not want to get back up.”**

and separately that the condition had **“worn down my psychological endurance”** and that he was **“reaching the point of not being able to push forward.”** See `CLIN-003627–003646`.

This was a GP-facing briefing. Do not attribute its contents to Neurology unless receipt by Neurology is separately established.

---

## 11. The longitudinal safety thread

The documentary sequence can now be stated without collapsing chronology:

> **2017:** balance “very bad”; patient believes a fall is imminent.
>
> **2019:** GP fall concern enters Neurology; clinician says the concern is understandable and that the patient might fall.
>
> **March 2023:** urgent referral records pain extreme enough to cause a “death wish”; GP asks for help before “trouble”; Parkinson's nurse records debilitating pain and no quality of life at 600 mg/day pregabalin.
>
> **2023 clinic:** major preceding functional collapse remains under review; gait assessment directly observes stumbles.
>
> **10 March 2025:** Neurology records “I won’t live with it” as the patient's way of expressing the intolerability of the foot-pain situation.
>
> **18 March 2025:** GP briefing explicitly joins fear of falling with the thought that, if a fall occurred, the patient might not want to get back up.

This does **not** establish one continuous suicidal state.

It does support:

> **a recurrent longitudinal connection between pain, mobility, falling and psychological endurance.**

---

## 12. Why this may be a second failure-of-integration question

The 2017 problem is principally:

> **integrated phenotype → incomplete construction → Protest → directional closure / partition.**

The 2023 problem may be structurally different but related:

> **pain crisis + mobility failure + medication complexity + OFF/dystonic possibilities + observed instability + psychological-endurance warning → multiple branch analyses.**

The question is whether the **whole crisis object** remained visible after those branches were separated.

The safe formulation is:

> **2023 may represent a second integration problem: not whether the founding foot mechanism was solved, but whether the patient's contemporaneous pain, mobility, falls and psychological-safety state was recognised and managed as a single risk object.**

That proposition requires expert assessment before becoming a breach allegation.

---

## 13. Evidential discipline

Do not infer from this layer that:

- the patient was continuously suicidal from 2023 to 2025;
- “death wish,” “I won’t live with it,” or “might not want to get back up” each proves suicidal intent or plan;
- observed stumbles prove an inevitable fall;
- one brief gait examination can quantify falls probability;
- the absence of a documented falls intervention proves none occurred;
- a particular falls-prevention intervention was legally required without expert evidence;
- alcohol, cannabis or pregabalin caused the observed stumbles;
- a fall actually occurred because of the 2023 state;
- the 2023 crisis proves the 2017 foot mechanism.

The supportable proposition is narrower:

> **The neurological record contained a recognised history of gait/balance risk, received an urgent 2023 pain crisis containing death/endurance language, directly observed stumbles during the subsequent clinical review, and documented major functional restriction. The patient-facing consultation presently shows extensive branch-specific diagnostic and medication reasoning but no identified falls-focused response or explicit integration of the physical, functional and psychological risk state.**

---

## 14. Controlling questions

For source audit:

> **Where is the documented response to the stumbles observed in 2023?**

> **Was any formal falls-risk, balance, physiotherapy, assistive-device or safety assessment undertaken, and if so where is it recorded?**

> **What was the clinician's contemporaneous understanding of the March “death wish” / “no quality of life” information by the time of the later consultation?**

> **Was the combined pain–mobility–falls–psychological state ever explicitly assessed as one safety problem?**

For expert evidence:

> **What assessment and intervention would ordinarily be expected when a patient with established Parkinsonian gait disorder, major current walking restriction and directly observed stumbles presents in the context of this pain and medication history?**

The shortest formulation is:

> **The 2023 gait examination did not merely fail to reconstruct the focal foot mechanics. It produced a positive finding of instability inside an active pain and mobility crisis. The unresolved question is what the record did with that finding.**
